# Chapter 002: Proust and Faulkner

This chapter begins from Recherche. Faulkner enters only as comparative pressure.

The aim is not to make Proust and Faulkner into a general modernist pair. The aim is to sharpen one Proustian perception: Recherche disturbs chronological time, but its disturbance often works toward unfolding, recovery, and reconfiguration rather than fracture as an end in itself.

## Lab Rule

Begin with Proust. Use Faulkner to amplify a difference. Return to Proust with a sharper sense of temporal unfolding.

If Faulkner becomes the center, stop and return to the Recherche anchor.

## 1. Recherche Anchor

Source:

- Marcel Proust, *In Search of Lost Time*
- Volume: *Swann's Way*
- Section: Combray
- Passage field: involuntary memory around taste, bodily sensation, and the return of a forgotten world

Short cue:

- a present sensation opens a past world that could not be recovered by deliberate memory.

Original text coordinate:

- English Proust source: [Proust, *Swann's Way*, translated by C. K. Scott Moncrieff, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7178/pg7178-images.html)
- Passage boundary: tea, madeleine, failed voluntary recall, and the expansion of Combray from sensation.
- Proust excerpt: "And suddenly the memory returns."
- Use note: English translation is the primary comparative object here; French may be checked later for source-language calibration.

Reading pressure:

- the past does not appear as information.
- the past returns through sensation.
- the scene unfolds gradually from bodily trigger to world-recovery.
- time becomes layered, but the layers are reorganized into intelligibility.

## 2. Proustian Pressure

Mark the passage as a structure of temporal unfolding.

The narrator does not control the return of the past by will. The body receives a sign before the mind can name it. Recognition comes later, and when it comes, it changes the status of the present.

The reading problem:

- How does Proust make time unstable without making instability the final form?

The first answer should come from passage movement:

- sensory trigger
- failed voluntary recall
- repeated attempt
- gradual emergence
- expansion of a buried world
- retrospective intelligibility

## 3. Comparative Pressure: Faulkner

Comparative author:

- William Faulkner

Work field:

- *The Sound and the Fury*

Passage field:

- fractured consciousness in which past and present collide without stable transition.

Original text coordinate:

- English source: [Faulkner, *The Sound and the Fury*, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/75170/pg75170-images.html)
- Passage boundary: Benjy opening section, fence, golf course, sensory trigger, and unstable temporal return.
- Faulkner excerpt: "Through the fence"
- Use note: compare the movement of this English prose directly with Scott Moncrieff's Proust.

Comparable English text:

| Text | Short excerpt | First movement to mark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proust in English | "And suddenly the memory returns." | sensation becomes recovered time |
| Faulkner | "Through the fence" | perception begins through obstruction |

Comparison prompt:

- Do not treat Proust as French content awaiting explanation. Compare the English prose pressures: return versus obstruction, unfolding recognition versus fractured access.

Contrast function:

- Faulkner clarifies temporal rupture: memory appears as break, collision, recursive wound, and disordered return.
- Proust clarifies temporal unfolding: memory disturbs chronology, but the disturbance can become a method of recovery.

Why this helps Recherche:

- Faulkner makes rupture visible as pressure.
- Returning to Proust, the reader can see that Proustian nonlinearity often seeks a different result: not only broken time, but rediscovered time.

## 4. Stylistic Matrix

| Field | Proust | Faulkner | Induction Product |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sentence movement | Follows sensation through delay into recognition | Breaks continuity and forces temporal collision | unfolding delay versus fractured recursion |
| Temporal logic | Past returns through present sensation and becomes legible | Past erupts into present without stable mediation | recovered time versus ruptured time |
| Perceptual regime | The reader waits for the sign to disclose its world | The reader reconstructs amid breakage | emergence versus dislocation |
| Rhythm | Slow pressure, repeated attempt, gradual release | Abrupt pressure, recurrence, interruption | unfolding rhythm versus broken rhythm |
| Return to Recherche | Proust's temporal instability becomes a path toward recognition. | Faulkner makes rupture visible by contrast. | Proustian temporal unfolding |

## 5. Induction Tasks

Do these in order. Do not begin with abstraction.

1. Mark the present sensory trigger in the Proust field.
2. Mark where deliberate memory fails.
3. Mark where repetition matters: the narrator must try again before recognition stabilizes.
4. Identify the moment when the past begins to become world, not merely recollection.
5. In the Faulkner field, mark how past and present collide rather than unfold.
6. Compare the two temporal movements: where does Faulkner fracture sequence, and where does Proust let the sign open a path?
7. Write one sentence naming the difference without saying only "nonlinear time."

## 6. Structural Restatement

Use this frame after completing the marks:

```text
In the Recherche anchor, time is not linear, but it is not merely broken. A present sensation unsettles the present, defeats voluntary memory, and then opens a buried world. Faulkner's ruptured time helps reveal that Proustian time often moves through disturbance toward unfolding recognition.
```

Do not copy the frame as a final answer. Rewrite it with evidence from the passage.

## 7. Comparative Paragraph

Faulkner helps the reader feel what temporal rupture can do when past and present collide without stable mediation. Against that pressure, Proust's involuntary memory becomes more precise. Recherche also breaks ordinary chronology, but the break is not the final structure. A present sensation interrupts the present, resists deliberate explanation, and gradually opens a buried world. The reader does not simply learn that the past returns. The reader learns that Proustian time can become readable through a slow sequence of bodily sign, failure, repetition, and recognition.

## 9. Return to Recherche

Return to the Proust anchor and answer:

- What does the body know before the mind knows?
- Where does deliberate memory fail?
- How does the past become a world rather than a remembered fact?
- What structure of temporal unfolding becomes visible after the Faulkner comparison?

Final induction:

Proustian time is unstable, but its instability can become an instrument of recovery. Faulkner makes rupture visible; Proust makes unfolding recognition visible.
